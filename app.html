DIY Survival Tips

Everyday job-site tips for everyday life — the same sections that live on the Dismukes Recruiting website.

DIY — do it yourself, worker style

DD says: “Every skill in your hands is money nobody can take from you. Start small, do it safe, do it right.”

  • Skills that help at work: measuring and marking twice before you cut, taping and masking for paint, sanding and surface prep, patching a small hole, hanging and leveling, basic caulking, and keeping tools clean, sharp, and oiled.
  • Practice at home first: a scrap board, an old door, a spare wall corner — make your mistakes where they’re free, then bring the steady hands to the job site.
  • Show your work: take before-and-after photos of your own projects. A small album of clean, finished work speaks louder than a long story.
  • Safety is the skill under every skill: eye and ear protection, gloves for the task, power off and water off before you open anything up, and stop and ask when a job is over your head — that’s not weakness, that’s how pros stay working.
  • Grow one rung at a time: pick one new skill a month, learn it properly, add it to the list you tell an employer. That’s how a helper becomes a hand, and a hand becomes a lead.

Survival thinking & skills — getting through hard times

DD says: “Hard times don’t ask permission. We go one step at a time — today’s problem first. Can’t dodge it, ram it — in order.”

  • Think in steps, not avalanches: write down the one most urgent thing — tonight’s sleep, tomorrow’s meal, the next call — and handle that first. A short list you finish beats a long one that freezes you.
  • Keep your papers together: ID, important numbers, and any documents you’d need for work or help, in one folder or one phone note you can reach fast.
  • A small go-bag: water, a snack that keeps, a change of clothes, phone charger, basic first aid, and any medicine you rely on — packed before you need it, in the truck or by the door.
  • Stay connected: keep two or three people you can honestly call. Pride is expensive; a phone call is cheap. Let people help the way you’d help them.
  • Protect the basics: sleep when you can, eat real food when you can, and be careful with heat, cold, and weather — check the forecast and dress for the night, not the noon.
  • Money triage: food, safe sleep, fuel or fare to work, then the rest — in that order — and tell anyone you owe the truth early. Most doors stay open longer for honest people.
